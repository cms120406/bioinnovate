Part 1

What I Need to Know

This last lesson is about the pressure that keeps blood moving: how it is measured, what sets it, and how the body keeps it in a safe range. It completes the work toward the General Biology 2 competency on transport and circulation (STEM_BIO11/12-IVa-h-1).

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  1. explain what the two numbers in a blood pressure reading mean;
  2. explain how cardiac output, resistance and blood volume set the blood pressure;
  3. describe the baroreceptor reflex as an example of negative feedback;
  4. explain how hormones and the kidneys change blood pressure;
  5. describe how blood flow is redistributed during exercise.

Part 5

What Is It

Five short readings. Readings 1 and 2 are for Session 7, and Readings 3 to 5 for Session 8.

Reading 1 of 5

Reading the numbers

is the force that blood exerts on the walls of the blood vessels. When people speak of “blood pressure”, they usually mean the pressure in the large arteries of the systemic circuit. It is measured in millimeters of mercury, written mm Hg.

The pressure in the arteries rises and falls with each heartbeat. The highest pressure, the , is reached when the ventricles contract and push blood into the arteries. The lowest pressure, the , is reached while the ventricles relax and refill. A reading is written as systolic over diastolic, for example 120/80 mm Hg.

Why doesn’t the pressure fall to zero between beats? The elastic walls of the large arteries are stretched during systole. During diastole they spring back and keep pressing on the blood, as you saw in Lesson 2. This is why the diastolic pressure stays well above zero.

Two useful values can be worked out from a reading. The is the difference between the two numbers. The is the average pressure that drives blood into the tissues during one beat. It is closer to the diastolic value, because the heart spends more of each beat relaxing than contracting (Betts et al., 2022, Section 20.2).

Pulse pressure = systolic − diastolic

Mean arterial pressure ≈ diastolic + (pulse pressure ÷ 3)

For 120/80 mm Hg, the pulse pressure is 40 mm Hg, and the mean arterial pressure is about 80 + 40 ÷ 3 ≈ 93 mm Hg.

Quick check

A blood pressure reading is 118/76 mm Hg. What is the pulse pressure?

Reading 2 of 5

Taking a reading

Blood pressure is measured with a and a stethoscope. A cuff is wrapped around the upper arm at about the level of the heart and pumped up until it squeezes the artery shut. Air is then let out slowly while the listener hears the artery just below the cuff (Betts et al., 2022, Section 20.2).

  • While the cuff pressure is above the systolic pressure, the artery stays closed. There is no sound.
  • When the cuff pressure falls just below the systolic pressure, blood spurts through with each beat. Tapping sounds begin, and the gauge shows the systolic pressure.
  • When the cuff pressure falls below the diastolic pressure, the artery stays open all the time and the flow is smooth again. The sounds stop, and the gauge shows the diastolic pressure.

Reading 3 of 5

What sets the pressure

Three things set the pressure in the arteries (Betts et al., 2022, Section 20.2). The first is : how much blood the heart pumps into the arteries each minute. The second is : how hard it is for blood to leave the arteries through the arterioles. Narrow arterioles give high resistance. The third is blood volume: how much blood fills the system. When more blood returns to the heart, more is pumped out with each beat, so the cardiac output rises.

Mean arterial pressure ≈ cardiac output × resistance

Think of a garden hose. The pressure in the hose rises if you open the tap further, so more water flows in. It also rises if you partly block the nozzle with your thumb, so the water meets more resistance on its way out. The heart is the tap, and the arterioles are the nozzle.

Of the three, the arterioles act the most quickly and the most strongly. Because resistance depends on the radius to the fourth power, a small narrowing of the arterioles all over the body raises the pressure sharply, without the heart doing anything different. This is why blood pressure is not just a matter of the heart.

Quick check

Two people have the same cardiac output. Person X has narrower arterioles than person Y. How do their mean arterial pressures compare?

Reading 4 of 5

Keeping pressure steady: the baroreceptor reflex

The body keeps its mean arterial pressure within a narrow range, an example of . If the pressure is too low, not enough blood reaches the brain and a person may faint. If it stays too high, it damages the walls of the vessels. The fastest control is a reflex that acts within seconds.

are stretch sensors in the walls of the aorta and of the , which supply the brain. When the pressure rises, these walls stretch more and the baroreceptors send signals more often. The signals go to the cardiovascular center in the (Betts et al., 2022, Section 20.4).

The center responds through nerves to the heart and the arterioles. When pressure has risen, it slows the heart, weakens its contractions and lets the arterioles widen, so the pressure falls back. When pressure has fallen, it does the opposite: the heart beats faster and harder and the arterioles narrow, so the pressure rises back.

In both cases the response reverses the change that started it. This makes the reflex an example of , with the same parts as every feedback loop: a change, sensors, a control center, effectors, and a result that undoes the change.

Reading 5 of 5

Slower controls, and pressure during exercise

Nerves act in seconds. and the kidneys act over minutes to days. They work mainly by changing how much water the body keeps, and so they change the blood volume (Betts et al., 2022, Section 20.4).

Most of the hormones in Table 4.3 act through the kidneys, so the urinary system and the endocrine system both take part in controlling blood pressure. Notice also that the heart is not only a pump. When its atria are stretched by too much blood, they release a hormone that makes the kidneys pass more sodium and water into the urine.

Exercise is a special case. Working muscles need far more oxygen, so two things happen together. Cardiac output rises, and blood is redirected: arterioles in the working muscles widen, while arterioles in the digestive organs and the kidneys narrow (Clark et al., 2018, Section 40.4). Because the muscle arterioles widen so much, the total resistance falls, and the mean arterial pressure rises only a little even though the output may triple.

Quick check

During hard exercise, cardiac output can rise to more than three times its resting value, yet the mean arterial pressure rises only a little. Which explanation fits best?

Blood pressure depends on cardiac output and on resistance, and blood volume affects it too. Even college students often find it hard to connect cardiac output, resistance and pressure (Michael et al., 2002).

Part 7

What I Have Learned

A summary of the lesson, and a second look at your predictions.

Key ideas

  • Blood pressure is the force of blood on the vessel walls. A reading gives the systolic pressure over the diastolic pressure.
  • Pulse pressure = systolic − diastolic. Mean arterial pressure ≈ diastolic + pulse pressure ÷ 3.
  • Mean arterial pressure ≈ cardiac output × resistance. Blood volume changes the pressure by changing the cardiac output.
  • A small change in arteriole diameter gives a large change in resistance and pressure.
  • The baroreceptor reflex corrects changes in pressure within seconds. It is negative feedback: sensors, control center, effectors, and a result that reverses the change.
  • Hormones and the kidneys adjust blood pressure more slowly, mainly through blood volume.
  • In exercise, cardiac output rises and blood is redirected to the working muscles.

Part 8

What I Can Do

Use the lesson at home.

Blood pressure that stays too high over the years raises the risk of heart attack, stroke and kidney disease (Betts et al., 2022, Section 20.4). In the Philippines, the 2018 national nutrition survey found hypertension in about 19 percent of adults aged 20 to 59 and in about 35 percent of adults aged 60 and older (Ona et al., 2021).

The Philippine guidelines advise these habits for people with high blood pressure, and they are good habits for everyone: eat less salt; eat more vegetables and fruits; be physically active; keep a healthy body weight; stop smoking; and avoid alcohol or drink only a little (Ona et al., 2021).

Explain three habits to someone at home

Choose three of the habits above. For each one, write one or two sentences that explain how it helps blood pressure, using what you learned in this lesson: cardiac output, resistance, blood volume, the kidneys or the arterioles.

Then write one sentence you could say to an adult at home to encourage one of these habits.

This is a science explanation, not medical advice. Anyone with a high reading should see a health worker.
