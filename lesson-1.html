Part 1

What I Need to Know

This lesson is about the heart: how it is built, how one heartbeat happens, and how much blood it pumps. It works toward the General Biology 2 competency of comparing transport and circulation in plants and animals (STEM_BIO11/12-IVa-h-1).

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  1. name the chambers, valves and large vessels of the heart and state what each one does;
  2. explain how the thickness of each chamber wall fits its job;
  3. describe what the chambers and valves do during one heartbeat;
  4. calculate cardiac output and predict how it changes;
  5. compare the hearts of fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

Part 3

What’s In

A quick look back at what you already know about circulation.

In Grade 9 Science, you learned that the circulatory system has three parts: the heart, the blood vessels and the blood. You also learned that it works with the respiratory system. Oxygen enters the blood in the lungs, and the blood carries it to every cell. The blood then carries carbon dioxide back to the lungs.

Blood carries more than gases. It carries nutrients from the digestive system, wastes to the kidneys, from glands to their targets, and heat from active muscles to the skin. None of this moves by itself. Something has to push the blood, and keep pushing it, every second of your life. That is the job of the heart.

Part 5

What Is It

Five short readings. Each has one picture or simulation and one quick check. Readings 1 and 2 are for Session 1, and Readings 3 to 5 for Session 2.

Reading 1 of 5

Four chambers, two pumps

Your heart is a hollow muscle about the size of your fist. It lies in the chest between the two lungs, a little to the left of the middle, with its tip pointing down and to the left. Most of its wall is , a kind of muscle found only in the heart. At rest it contracts about 75 times a minute, or about 100,000 times a day (Betts et al., 2022, Section 19.1).

Inside are four chambers. The two upper chambers, the , receive blood that returns to the heart. The two lower chambers, the , pump blood out of the heart. A wall of muscle called the runs down the middle and keeps the right side apart from the left side.

So the heart is really two pumps side by side. The right side receives oxygen-poor blood from the body through the two and pumps it to the lungs through the pulmonary trunk. The left side receives oxygen-rich blood from the lungs through the and pumps it to the whole body through the .

The two pumps work at the same time. Both atria contract together, and then both ventricles contract together. With every beat, the right ventricle sends blood toward the lungs while the left ventricle sends blood toward the body. This is why the heart is called a double pump.

How to read a heart diagram

A heart diagram shows the heart of a person who is facing you. The person’s right side is on your left, the same as when you look at a classmate. In every diagram in this module, the right atrium and right ventricle are on the left side of the picture.

Reading 2 of 5

Thick walls, thin walls, and four valves

Each chamber wall is as thick as its job requires. The atria only push blood a short way down into the ventricles, so their walls are thin. The right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs, which are close by and offer little resistance. The left ventricle must push blood through the whole body, from the brain to the toes, so its wall is by far the thickest and it produces the highest pressure (Betts et al., 2022, Section 19.1).

Even so, both ventricles pump the same volume of blood with each beat. The left ventricle does not pump more blood. It pumps the same amount at a much higher pressure.

Blood must move through the heart in one direction only, and four valves make sure that it does. Two lie between the atria and the ventricles: the on the right and the on the left. Two lie at the exits of the ventricles: the pulmonary valve on the right and the aortic valve on the left.

A valve has no muscle of its own. Its flaps are pushed open when the pressure behind them is higher, and pushed shut when the pressure in front of them is higher. Thin cords called tie the flaps of the atrioventricular valves to the in the ventricle wall. The cords do not pull the valves open. They only stop the flaps from turning inside out into the atria when the ventricles contract.

A valve can fail in two ways. In , the opening is narrowed, so the chamber behind it must push harder. In , the valve does not close fully, so some blood leaks backward each beat (Betts et al., 2022, Section 19.1).

Reading 3 of 5

One heartbeat: the cardiac cycle

One complete heartbeat is called the . In each cycle, every chamber relaxes and fills, which is called , and then contracts and empties, which is called . At a resting rate of 75 beats a minute, one cycle lasts about 0.8 second (Betts et al., 2022, Section 19.3).

Each beat starts by itself. A small group of cells in the wall of the right atrium, the , produces an electrical signal without any command from the brain. The signal spreads over both atria, so they contract. It then pauses for a moment at the before it spreads over the ventricles. The pause gives the atria time to empty before the ventricles contract (Clark et al., 2018, Section 40.3).

Most of the filling happens without any pushing at all. While the whole heart is relaxed, blood flows from the veins through the atria and the open atrioventricular valves into the ventricles. This passive flow gives about 70 to 80 percent of the filling. The atria then contract and add the last 20 to 30 percent.

When the ventricles contract, the pressure inside them rises. The atrioventricular valves are pushed shut, and this makes the first heart sound, “lub”. For a moment all four valves are closed and no blood moves. When the pressure is high enough, the semilunar valves open and blood rushes into the arteries. When the ventricles relax, the semilunar valves are pushed shut, which makes the second sound, “dub” (Betts et al., 2022, Section 19.3).

Reading 4 of 5

How much blood the heart pumps

is the volume of blood that one ventricle pumps in one minute. It depends on two things: how many times the heart beats in a minute, the , and how much blood one ventricle pumps with each beat, the .

Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume

For an adult at rest, the heart rate is about 75 beats per minute and the stroke volume is about 70 mL. So the cardiac output is 75 × 70 = 5,250 mL per minute, or about 5.25 L per minute. An adult has about 5 L of blood, so at rest about as much blood as the whole body holds passes through each side of the heart every minute (Betts et al., 2022, Section 19.4).

During hard exercise, cardiac output can rise to four or five times its resting value. Both factors rise. Nerves and the hormone epinephrine make the heart beat faster, and the heart also beats more forcefully while more blood returns to it, so the stroke volume grows too.

Training changes the balance between the two. Regular exercise makes the heart muscle stronger, so a trained athlete pumps more blood with each beat. Because the stroke volume is larger, the same resting cardiac output needs fewer beats, and the resting heart rate of an athlete can be well below 60 beats per minute.

Quick check

After months of training, a swimmer’s resting heart rate drops from 72 to 56 beats per minute. Her resting cardiac output stays at about 5 L per minute. What must have changed?

Reading 5 of 5

Hearts across the animal kingdom

Not every animal keeps its blood inside vessels. In insects and most mollusks, the heart pumps a fluid into open spaces around the organs, where it bathes the cells directly. This is an . In earthworms and all vertebrates, blood always stays inside the heart and the vessels. This is a . A closed system takes more energy to run, but it can deliver blood quickly and under pressure to very active tissues (Clark et al., 2018, Section 40.1).

All vertebrates have a closed system, but their hearts differ in the number of chambers. A fish heart has two chambers, one atrium and one ventricle. Blood passes through the heart only once on each trip around the body, so fish have . The blood loses much of its pressure in the gill capillaries before it reaches the rest of the body.

Amphibians, such as frogs, have three chambers: two atria and one ventricle. Oxygen-rich blood from the lungs and oxygen-poor blood from the body enter the same ventricle, so some mixing happens. Most reptiles also have three chambers, but their ventricle is partly divided, so there is less mixing. Crocodiles and alligators have four chambers (Clark et al., 2018, Section 40.1).

In birds and mammals, the heart has four chambers and the two ventricles are fully separated. Oxygen-rich blood never mixes with oxygen-poor blood. The left ventricle can send blood to the body at high pressure while the right ventricle sends blood to the delicate lungs at low pressure. This efficient delivery of oxygen supports the high energy needs of animals that keep their bodies warm.

Quick check

An adult frog has two atria but only one ventricle. What is the main consequence for the blood that the ventricle sends to the body?

Check these ideas

Many learners hold the two ideas below. Decide whether you agree with each one, then see what science says.

Common idea

“The heart cleans the blood.”

What do you think?

What science says

The heart is a pump. It moves blood but does not change it. Carbon dioxide leaves the blood in the lungs, and other wastes are filtered out by the kidneys. This idea has been recorded among learners (Özgür, 2013).

Common idea

“Blood is made in the heart.”

What do you think?

What science says

Blood cells are made in the red bone marrow (Betts et al., 2022, Section 18.2). The heart only moves the blood. This idea has also been recorded among learners (Özgür, 2013).

Part 7

What I Have Learned

A summary of the lesson, and a second look at your predictions.

Key ideas

  • The heart has four chambers. The atria receive blood and the ventricles pump it out.
  • The right side pumps oxygen-poor blood to the lungs. The left side pumps oxygen-rich blood to the body. The septum keeps the two apart.
  • Wall thickness matches the work done. The left ventricle has the thickest wall, yet both ventricles pump the same volume.
  • Valves open and close because of pressure differences, and they keep blood moving one way. “Lub” is the atrioventricular valves closing. “Dub” is the semilunar valves closing.
  • The SA node starts each beat. Most ventricular filling happens while the heart is relaxed; the atria add the last part.
  • Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume. It is about 5 L per minute at rest.
  • Fish have two chambers and single circulation. Birds and mammals have four chambers that fully separate oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood.

What I Can Do

Estimate your own cardiac output

1. Sit quietly for two minutes. Count your pulse at the wrist for 30 seconds and multiply by 2. This is your resting heart rate.

2. If you are able to, do jumping jacks or march in place for one minute, then count your pulse again right away. Skip this step if you have a health condition or feel unwell, and use a classmate’s numbers instead.
